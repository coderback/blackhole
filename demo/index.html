Black Hole

Light rays integrated through curved spacetime, once per pixel, in your GPU. This is a WebGL port of the C++/OpenGL version, which uses compute shaders that browsers do not offer. Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom.

How it works

Every pixel fires a ray from the camera and steps it along the Schwarzschild geodesic equations in spherical coordinates, written as first-order equations in the coordinates and their derivatives. Each step checks for the event horizon, the two reference spheres, the jet cones and a crossing of the disk plane. The equations, disk and jet colouring, tone mapping and feature presets come straight from shaders/geodesic.comp and src/black_hole.cpp. Everything runs live in your browser, with no saved frames.

What changed in the port

Each difference is marked in the comments of geodesic.frag.

  • Compute to fragment. WebGL2 has no compute shaders, so the ray tracer runs in a fragment shader over a full-screen triangle.
  • Integrator. The C++ function named rk4Step takes one forward Euler step of 107 m (about 0.0008 rs) per iteration, with a budget of 32,000 to 256,000 iterations per ray. The port integrates the same right-hand sides with true fourth-order Runge-Kutta, at a step proportional to r that shrinks near rs, in 90 to 450 steps depending on quality.
  • Per-ray frame. Each ray gets a polar axis perpendicular to its starting plane. The equations are unchanged by the rotation, and it keeps long steps away from the singularity at the coordinate pole.
  • Jet sampling. Jet brightness is scaled by step length so it adds up the same along a path. One time-varying jet term moved about 1000 radians per C++ step, which averages to a constant, so the port uses that constant.
  • Starfield. Added here so the bending of light is visible. The C++ leaves escaped rays black.
  • Left out. The test-object gravity mode (right mouse button) and the spacetime grid mesh.

Physics limits, stated plainly

  • The radial equation. The C++ radial equation ends in r * (dθ² + sin²θ dφ²). The Schwarzschild Christoffel symbol gives (r − rs) * (...). As written, light bends about a third as much as general relativity predicts: 0.0340 rad against 0.1063 rad with the corrected term at b = 20 rs over the same path, and a shadow edge near 1.74 rs rather than the analytic 2.60 rs (the corrected term gives 2.58 rs from this starting distance). The page opens with the equations as written in the C++. The checkbox applies the correction so you can compare.
  • Kerr mode is not a full Kerr solution. It keeps the Schwarzschild equations, uses the Kerr formula for the horizon radius, and adds a small Lense-Thirring style term to the azimuthal acceleration about the z axis, while the disk lies in the y = 0 plane. It also makes the disk emission asymmetric. The Kerr metric functions in the GLSL are not used by the integration. The shadow changes little with spin: its edge moves from 1.75 rs to 1.71 rs at a/M = 0.8.
  • The Doppler term is nearly invisible. It is ported as written. Its velocity works out near β ≈ 10−5, and it follows a fixed world direction rather than the viewer, so toggling it shows almost no change.
  • Colours are artistic gradients. The disk uses colour ramps with redshift-style scaling, not radiative transfer, and the disk is a flat plane that stops a ray on contact. The temperature value only selects a ramp.
  • TON618 mode is a feature preset. It changes the disk and jet settings. The mass and rs stay those of Sgr A*, as in the C++.
